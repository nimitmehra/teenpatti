Colour study, for choosing

Royal colours, chosen by rule, then by eye.

Blues, greens, golds, beiges and greys. Each family is drawn out into eleven shades; a simple rule picks the right shade for each job, so text is always easy to read; then you choose the combination you like best.

How I chose them

  1. Start from royal things, not from a colour wheel.Sapphire and lapis blue, emerald and jade, gold leaf and saffron, parchment and ivory, slate and stone. These are the families.
  2. Draw each family out into eleven even shades.From the palest (50) to the darkest (950). The shades are made with a colour model that matches how the eye sees, so each step looks equally different from the next.
  3. Give every job a rule, and let the rule pick the shade.Main button and header band: the lightest blue where white text still has at least 7 to 1 contrast (the strictest reading standard). Good-news and caution text: the lightest green or red with at least 5 to 1 on white. Gold: never for text on white (it is too faint); only for thin lines, a pale highlight behind the current player, and lettering on the dark blue band, where it reads clearly.
  4. Put the result on a real screen and judge by eye.Below, every palette is applied to the same turn screen and scoreboard. The numbers next to each one are the contrast checks. One nudge was made by eye: the rule picked a red that looked coral, so caution moved one shade deeper to a true garnet.

Three palettes on the same screen

Same fonts, same layout; only the colours change. The current player’s seat is highlighted in the pale gold of each palette.

The shades behind the recommendation

Each row is one family, palest to darkest. Outlined shades are the ones the rules picked. The small number is contrast against white: 4.5 or more is readable text, 7 or more is the strict standard.
